Product Design

Human–AI interaction · Enterprise SaaS · Legal tech

Overview

LEXAFIDE is an American legal-tech company that builds AI agents and call-handling tools for personal- injury law firms. It connects telephony, AI voice agents and the firm's CRM, Litify, into one operating layer.

I joined as a UI/UX design intern while the company was scaling fast, then stayed on part-time. My work moved from single screens to the question underneath all of them: what should the AI do, and where should a person decide?

ROLE

TIMELINE

GOAL

How might we reduce the decisions a legal team has to make, without removing the ones that need their judgement?

OUTCOME

Onboarding direction validated with two client-firm managers. The AI voicemail agent limited to a fixed scope for compliance. Call Intelligence, notifications panel and the Comms Log navigation deployed.

And designing for people I'd never met

My users were law-firm employees in America. I was working remotely from India, I had never met them, and I didn't know their mental models.

Almost everything I knew about their day came through other people: my mentor and supervisor Tejas, team members based in the US, and two moderated sessions with firm managers. (And Suits, which Tejas assured us was a fairly accurate picture.)

Even vocabulary depended on the data underneath. Whether a field should say "Party ID" or "Client ID" only made sense once I understood how Litify structures its records.

So some assumptions, like how comfortable users were with technology and what their day looked like outside calls, had to be made and then tested.

Onboarding

A form? Or a first conversation?

Most people who would log into Lexafide never chose to. Their firm mandated it, and they arrived sceptical.

So onboarding wasn't about getting someone signed up. It was about getting a mandated user to trust the platform enough to use it. I owned it end to end: the flow, two prototypes, the testing protocol and the sessions.

Testing with two client-firm managers

Before the sessions I wrote down the riskiest assumptions and hypotheses, a lean principle that puts validated learning ahead of exhaustive coverage. In the sessions: never affirm a participant mid-task, and ask "why" more than once.

The hardest part was staying quiet. Not filling silence, not reassuring a confused participant. It felt unnatural in the first session and closer to instinct by the second.

The same sessions also tested the Active Call prototype (next section).

01

The card layout felt more focused and optimised than the split screen.

Changed: the card-based direction went forward.

02

The value-proposition screens delayed login without adding value.

Changed: they were removed.

03

It still explained too little about what Lexafide would do for them day to day.

Changed: A single value proposition card was added for each role

Active Call

Somebody's whole conversation.

The user journey and visual design were by my co-intern, Vasuman. I contributed design feedback, co-ran user tests, and worked on the redesign based on their feedback.

Contributed

Tested with clients

Mistakes here happen during a real conversation with a real client, so the screen has to be ready for anything. A husband calls from his wife's number, so both party records have to load. One caller has several open matters. And Lexa, Lexafide's AI, is processing the call and giving guidance in real time.

Testing showed the key feature was pulling scattered Litify data into one panel.

Lexa detects two open matters and asks to confirm one before loading sensitive details.

The most praised detail: the handoff brief

Before someone accepts a transferred call, they see a short brief: who's calling, what's been discussed and what's still open. The next person starts warm instead of reconstructing the conversation.

And the moment that changed the brief

In the prototype, the agent disclosed a low settlement offer. Case managers and paralegals are barred from giving legal advice. It exposed a blind spot in how we'd scoped "proactive" AI.

THE TURN

We had designed around what the agent could do,

before asking what it should be allowed to say.

From here, AI support stopped meaning "help as much as possible" and started meaning "make its own limits visible by default" (Amershi et al., 2019).

More automation

More judgement

AI acts

Inside a fixed, agreed scope

AI suggests

Proposes; nothing changes yet

Human reviews

Sees what the AI found and how sure it is

Human edits

Corrects before anything is saved

Human decides

Owns the outcome

How might we

reduce the number of decisions a legal team has to make, without removing the ones that need their judgement?

03

PIP · picture-in-picture

Designing within what can be built now.

Co-created

In development

PIP keeps Lexafide with you while you work somewhere else. It was one of the hardest problems of the internship, not because of the visuals, but because the product's scope and the technical limits were both unclear at the same time.

The challenge wasn't imagining what PIP could do. It was defining what PIP could realistically do now.

Where we started

Lexafide was bringing together several ways of working with calls, automation and AI. But the existing experience didn't clearly distinguish what each capability was for

Command Used for automated calls but it is not understandable

Prompt, Workflow and Agent Different ways to run automated calls, but their distinction and relationship was unclear

Phone A dialer for making calls is unnecessary on a PC

PIP The functionality is currently only used to make calls

Next, I mapped the technical boundary

Before designing anything, I sent Tejas scope questions.

Our research had found that picture-in-picture is meant mostly for media: video, calls and navigation, with the system owning size and placement.

So, is PIP a lightweight call companion, a minimised active- call interface, or a broader operational layer?

From my scope questions to the product lead

“

Feasible

Call duration, a standard timer

Escalation notifications, rendered from a backend event

Reminders and waiting states

Barge-in, if the telephony layer supports it

Feasible, with caveats

Live call controls need the telephony SDK reachable from the PIP window

Rolling transcript: two or three lines at most

Typing to Lexa is cramped; nudges from Lexa fit better

Case search works as find-and-select, not browsing

Hard limits

Mobile PiP isn't meant for arbitrary floating UI

Complex forms and buttons are a poor experience

The system, not the app, owns size and placement

Six roles, one small window

Different people answer calls with different goals. PIP couldn't simply expose everything to everyone.

Instead, I mapped what each role needs during a call, and what should remain in the full platform.

Insight

The value of PIP wasn't in fitting the whole platform into a smaller window. It was in deciding what didn't belong there.

Five rules followed

Presence without intrusion

Always available, never demanding.

Ambient intelligence

The default is observing and preparing, not prompting.

Action economy

Anything inside PIP finishes in under 3 seconds, or escalates.

Truthful minimalism

Show only what is confirmed. Never show inferred data as fact.

Escalation is a feature

"You need more space for this", not "we couldn't handle it".

214 iterations later

(a conservative estimate)

Every layout was critiqued before choosing.

Each direction was evaluated against usability, role needs and technical feasibility. The goal wasn't to find the most feature-rich version, but the smallest surface that could still be genuinely useful.

Tejas also called us out when our rapid prototyping rested on too many assumptions. My faculty advisor helped reframe it: design the near-term, buildable version in parallel with the long-term vision, and let user goals decide.

What changed

Rapid prototyping surfaced an important tension: some concepts solved imagined future workflows rather than problems Lexafide could support today.

Now, next, later

Tejas prioritised whatever earns most, so the scope was split into what can ship now and what belongs to the vision. Even the transfer handoff card from Active Call sits in the future tier here: great, but not buildable yet.

04

Comms Log

The shared memory of humans and agents.

Led

Navigation deployed

Rest in development

The Comms Log is the central record of every party relationship. Each call, its transcript, tasks and notes attach to a party through their number. It's where call history, AI-extracted case data, activity and tasks meet.

How do people retrieve the right history when a firm is handling thousands of calls?

Spot the differences

50+ issues, component by component

We broke the existing interface into organisms, molecules and atoms (Frost's atomic design) and critiqued each part, logging over 50 individual issues just across search, filters, pagination and the caller-info bars.

The critique covered top and side navigation, search, the conversation view, caller info and LLM mapping.

An overview of the critique.

NEW

Search Bar

Search first for active matters, pages for history

Search is the primary way back to a live matter so calls have been arranged according to last call, groupwise

Quick filters

Missed, Unknown and Inbound in one click; everything else in a dedicated panel.

Saved searches

Repeat lookups differ by role, so the common ones are one tap away.

"Load More" in batches of 20, not infinite scroll

Infinite scroll strips away the checkpoints people rely on to know where they are in a long list.

OLD

NEW

LLM Mapping

AI proposes. A human commits.

Choose the source

Only calls with useful information are suggested for extraction.

Compare, don't overwrite

The existing CRM value sits next to the AI's suggestion.

Confidence decides the status

Low-confidence fields go to manual review.

Apply once, knowingly.

"7 fields extracted · 5 ready · 2 require review · 0 conflicts."

OLD

Most of Lexafide's revenue comes from automated agent workflows rather than people reviewing the Comms Log, so it was deprioritised and I didn't get to user-test it. The new navigation has been deployed; the rest is in development. Task management was led by Vasuman (the task "iceberg" model); I contributed its escalation logic, and both a global and a conversation-level task manager shipped.

05Call Intelligence

Letting the firm decide what the AI writes.

SoloDeployed

Call Intelligence turns a call into a structured report for the team that needs it. The question shifted from "how can AI assist the user?" to "how can the user direct what the AI produces?"

How might legal teams control AI-written call reports without being exposed to the technical complexity underneath?

01System overview02Admin config, multi-template03Automation builder04Automated delivery05Manual run06Report preview

The AI suggests. The person picks.

  • A suggested call type, which the person can change. The report templates update to match.
  • A template for the audience: a concise intake summary, or a detailed report for attorneys.
  • An honest wait: an estimated time before anything runs.

Every report says what it is

  • Visible provenance: call type, template, model and prompt version on every report.
  • A stated limit: "This analysis assists — it does not replace — professional review."
  • Feedback with a destination: accuracy ratings go to the prompt owner, tied to the exact template and prompt version. Version history and re-run keep a bad report from being lost.

Failure is designed in

Automations that matched a call ("MVA Intake → Intake Team"), reports that are ready, generations that failed, model timeouts with a retry, and a provider slowing down: each is a state in the notification system, not a surprise.

06Multi-matter calls

When one call stops meaning one case.

SoloIn development

The first mental model was simple: one call, one matter. Real calls aren't like that.

One conversation can cover several matters, or start a new intake. It's the edge case from Active Call and the "9 matching records" from the Comms Log, finally treated as the normal case.

What is the object the interface is really representing, when a call's legal context isn't singular?

Before: 1 call → 1 matter

One record at a time, nothing left behind

  1. A multi-matter switch in the log-call dialog, with a count: "3 matters + 1 intake discussed".
  2. Add a matter or an intake the call touched but the system didn't catch.
  3. Record 1 of 2, each with its own parties, summary and logging options.
  4. "0 of 2 records logged", so it's obvious when something hasn't been saved.

Reflection

Reflection

What I learned

  • Testing showed an AI can be capable and still wrong for the moment: the agent revealed a settlement offer before an attorney had framed it, so limits have to be designed in from the start.
  • Working remotely, I understood US law-firm staff through other people, and learnt that even a label like "Party ID" or "Client ID" depends on how Litify structures its records.
  • I held designs back until they felt finished. Earlier, rougher handovers to engineering would have surfaced PIP's technical limits sooner.

What I’d do next

  • User-test the redesigned Comms Log, which was deprioritised before it could be validated.
  • Talk to firm staff informally and early, instead of relying on two moderated sessions as the only source of truth.
  • Measure whether self-serve onboarding reduces the manual setup Lexafide's team does for each new firm employee.
